\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This report analyses the predictive modelling of methylene blue (MB) dye
adsorption capacity (mg\,g$^{-1}$) onto activated carbon adsorbents using
two related datasets:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Dataset~4:} $N=56$ samples, 7 features (SSA, PV, PS,
        molar dye/AC concentration, pH, temperature, equilibrium time).
  \item \textbf{Dataset~5:} $N=48$ samples, 6 features (SSA, PV, MPD,
        dosage ratio, pH, temperature); equilibrium time removed.
\end{itemize}

For each dataset, the analysis follows a principled pipeline:
(1)~quantitative characterisation of the data to identify which ML model
families' assumptions align best with the data properties;
(2)~hyperparameter optimisation (HPO) across the top-5 candidate
architectures via 5-fold cross-validation;
(3)~final evaluation of the best architecture using 10-trial random
splits and leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV);
(4)~topological and Nyquist analyses for data collection recommendations.

The best model selected for Dataset~4 is \textbf{Gradient Boosting}
($n_{\text{est}}=30$, $\eta=0.07$, \texttt{max\_depth}=4,
\texttt{subsample}=0.8, \texttt{max\_features}=\texttt{sqrt}),
achieving a 10-trial mean RMSE of $311.0 \pm 127.7$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ and
LOO-CV RMSE of 368.0\,mg\,g$^{-1}$.
For Dataset~5, the best model is also \textbf{Gradient Boosting}
($n_{\text{est}}=30$, $\eta=0.1$, \texttt{max\_depth}=5,
\texttt{subsample}=1.0), achieving 10-trial RMSE of
$187.8 \pm 75.7$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ and LOO-CV RMSE of 220.8\,mg\,g$^{-1}$.

This report provides:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A systematic, quantitative model selection procedure grounded in
        dataset properties and model-family assumptions.
  \item Hyperparameter optimisation via grid search with 5-fold CV across
        five candidate model families.
  \item Evaluation of the best architecture with 10-trial random splits
        and LOO-CV, with quantitative diagnosis of performance variance.
  \item Paired statistical hypothesis testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank) for
        model comparison, with MAE as a complementary metric to RMSE.
  \item Residual diagnostics including heteroscedasticity tests,
        per-quartile bias analysis, and permutation-based feature importance.
  \item Learning-curve analysis indicating expected marginal gains from
        additional data.
  \item Topological and Nyquist analyses of under-sampled regions with
        concrete data collection recommendations.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
This report constitutes an \textbf{exploratory analysis} rather than a
definitive statistical study.  Key limitations include:
(i)~with only 56/48 samples, no model comparison achieves conventional
statistical significance ($p < 0.05$) between the two top-ranked models
(GB vs.\ RF);
(ii)~all evaluation is internal (CV-based); no external validation dataset
exists;
(iii)~point predictions are reported without prediction intervals or
epistemic/aleatoric uncertainty decomposition;
(iv)~RMSE under heavy target skew ($\gamma \approx 2.1$--$2.3$) is
dominated by a few high-value samples, making absolute RMSE values
fragile.
Conclusions should be interpreted as \emph{directionally correct} rather
than \emph{statistically conclusive}.

alternative.  Ridge and Lasso are included as baselines to confirm that
linear models are inadequate.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Gradient Boosting ranks first}, beating Random
        Forest by 4.6\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ on CV RMSE and 12.0 on 10-trial RMSE.
        However, a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 10 per-trial
        RMSEs yields $W=35$, $p=0.784$ (one-sided, $H_1$: GB $<$ RF),
        meaning the difference is \textbf{not statistically significant}.
        The same holds for MAE ($\Delta = +19.5$, $p = 0.920$).
        Both models perform comparably; the ranking is plausible but
        should not be interpreted as a definitive selection.
  \item \textbf{GB is significantly better than SVR, Ridge, and Lasso}
        (Wilcoxon $p < 0.02$ for all three on both RMSE and MAE), confirming
        that the feature--target relationship is fundamentally non-linear.
  \item \textbf{Linear models (Ridge, Lasso) are inadequate}: CV R$^2$ is
        near zero, confirming that the feature--target relationship is
        fundamentally non-linear.
  \item \textbf{SVR is intermediate}: its RBF kernel captures some
        non-linearity (R$^2 = 0.324$) but cannot match the
        tree-based ensembles at this sample size.
  \item The HPO-selected GB configuration ($n_\text{est}=30$,
        $\eta=0.07$, \texttt{max\_depth}=4, \texttt{subsample}=0.8,
        \texttt{max\_features}=\texttt{sqrt}) uses aggressive
        regularisation (low tree count, row subsampling) suited to the
        small training folds.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Caveat on model selection.}
Given the non-significant difference between GB and RF ($p = 0.784$), the
``best model'' designation is provisional.  With $n = 10$ trials and a
CV$_\text{RMSE} > 0.4$, the 95\,\% confidence interval for the mean RMSE
difference overlaps zero.  We proceed with GB for consistency with the
HPO ranking, but note that RF would be an equally defensible choice.

\section{Evaluation Methodology: 10-Trial Random Splits}
\label{sec:eval}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Protocol Description}

The evaluation protocol mirrors the stability-analysis notebook:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The 56 samples are first partitioned by \texttt{prepare.py} into
        train+val ($n=47$) and a fixed held-out test set ($n=9$).
  \item The train+val pool is then split 10 times with distinct random
        seeds (80\,\% train, 20\,\% test per trial).
  \item \texttt{val\_rmse} = mean test RMSE across 10 trials in original
        mg\,g$^{-1}$ units.
\end{itemize}

Each trial yields approximately 37 training samples and 10 test samples.
The fixed seeds used are:
\texttt{[521, 737, 740, 660, 411, 678, 626, 513, 859, 136]}, generated via
\texttt{numpy.random.RandomState(42).choice(1000, 10, replace=False)}.

\subsection{Quantitative Diagnosis of Performance Variance}

\paragraph{Observation 1: Extreme per-trial variance.}
Test R$^2$ ranges from $-0.208$ to 0.891 (coefficient of variation
CV$_\text{R2} = 0.831$), and test RMSE ranges from 150.9 to 551.3
(CV$_\text{RMSE} = 0.411$).  A 95\,\% bootstrap confidence interval for
mean RMSE is $(238.9, 389.3)$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ — a range that spans
$\pm 24$\,\% of the mean.  This width is comparable to the baseline
performance itself, making reliable model comparison extremely difficult
with the current dataset size.

\paragraph{Observation 2: Test set composition drives performance.}
Trials where the test set
contains high-valued samples (above the 90th percentile) exhibit
substantially higher RMSE, since the model has limited exposure to those
extreme conditions during training.  Specifically:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Seed 513 (test R$^2 = -0.208$): no high-value samples in test set,
        but the model overfits to extreme points in training and cannot
        generalise to the mid-range test samples.
  \item Seeds 521 and 136 (2 high-value samples in test): RMSE $>420$.
  \item Seeds 678 and 660 (0--1 high-value samples in test, but balanced
        mid-range): RMSE $<190$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Observation 3: Test set overlap (Jaccard similarity).}
Pairwise Jaccard similarity between test sets across the 10 trials is
$0.123 \pm 0.073$, close to the theoretical minimum for 10-out-of-47
subsets ($\approx 0.10$).  This confirms that the 10 trials sample
\emph{different} subsets of the data; however, low Jaccard does not imply
independence of \emph{errors} — the training data remains highly correlated
and the model may exhibit similar systematic biases across folds.

\paragraph{Observation 4: Training R$^2$ is nearly perfect.}
Across all trials, training R$^2 \in [0.953, 0.971]$ while test R$^2$
fluctuates between $-0.208$ and 0.891.  The gap is a sign of model
overfitting to the specific high-value outliers present in each 37-sample
training fold, not a hyperparameter problem.  Further regularisation
yielded no improvement because the problem is data-size-limited, not
complexity-limited.

\subsection{Heteroscedasticity}

A key reviewer concern is whether RMSE is appropriate under the skewed
target distribution.  We test for heteroscedasticity using the Spearman
correlation between $|r_i| = |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$ (LOO residuals) and the
true target $y_i$.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Heteroscedasticity tests — Dataset~4 (LOO residuals).}
\label{tab:ds4_hetero}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
Test & $\rho$ & $p$ & Interpretation \\
\midrule
Spearman($|r|$, $\hat{y}$) & 0.542 & 0.0001 & Significant \\
Spearman($|r|$, $y$)       & 0.327 & 0.025  & Significant \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Finding:}  The target exhibits \textbf{significant
heteroscedasticity} ($p < 0.05$): prediction errors grow with target
magnitude.  This means (a)~RMSE is disproportionately driven by
high-value samples, and (b)~MAE should be used as a complementary
metric to reduce the influence of extreme errors.

\subsection{Residual Distribution}

Shapiro--Wilk test for normality of residuals: $p = 0.0003$, indicating
\textbf{non-normal residuals}.  This means that parametric confidence
intervals (e.g., normal-theory CIs on the mean) should be interpreted
with caution; bootstrap CIs (used throughout this report) are more
appropriate.

\subsection{Power-Law Fit and Saturation}

Fitting a power law $\text{RMSE} \approx a \cdot n_\text{train}^{b}$
to the empirical curve gives:
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE}(n) \approx 644.1 \cdot n^{-0.166}, \quad r = -0.998
\end{equation}

This slow exponent ($b \approx -0.17$) indicates \textbf{early saturation}:
the model generalisation is already near the plateau achievable under the
current data distribution.  In contrast, data-rich regimes typically show
$b \approx -0.5$ (root-$n$ improvement).

Neither should be treated as the ``correct'' estimate of generalisation
error; they are complementary views.

of $314.1 \pm 186.1$ and $329.5 \pm 128.9$ respectively, providing a
third calibration point that is broadly consistent with both estimates.

\section{Quantitative Recommendations for Dataset Collection}
\label{sec:recommendations}

\subsection{Priority 1: Increase Coverage of High-Performance Region}

\textbf{Rationale.} The model's worst generalisation errors occur for
samples in the high-adsorption tail ($>$993\,mg\,g$^{-1}$), which is
the region of greatest practical interest.  Currently only 6 samples
(10.7\,\% of the dataset) occupy this region.

\textbf{Recommendation.} Add at least 15--20 new experiments with:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item SSA $> 1800$\,m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$
  \item Molar dye/AC concentration $> 3$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$
  \item Pore size 1.5--3.5\,nm (mesoporous range)
\end{itemize}
This would bring the top-decile sample count to $\approx 21$--26, and
based on the power-law model (Equation~1) would be expected to reduce
mean RMSE from 311 to approximately 270\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ if these samples
are well-distributed within the tail.

\subsection{Priority 2: Fill the Transition Quadrants}

\textbf{Rationale.} The high-SSA/low-conc and low-SSA/high-conc
quadrants each have only 6 samples (Table~\ref{tab:quadrant}), yet
they represent the non-trivial interaction region where the model must
interpolate.  The model's difficulty in these regions inflates RMSE in
trials where such samples appear in the test set.

\textbf{Recommendation.} Add at least 10 experiments per quadrant:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textit{High-SSA / Low-conc} ($>$920\,m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$,
        $<$0.88\,mg\,g$^{-1}$): $+$10 experiments
  \item \textit{Low-SSA / High-conc} ($<$920\,m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$,
        $>$0.88\,mg\,g$^{-1}$): $+$10 experiments
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Priority 3: Address Pore Size Outliers}

\textbf{Rationale.} PS is the second most important feature (importance =
0.276) but has 8 IQR outliers and skewness of 7.2, indicating 8 samples
at macropore sizes ($>$50\,nm) that are inconsistent with the rest of the
dataset (mesopore-dominated: 2--4\,nm).  These outliers disproportionately
influence tree-based splits.

\textbf{Recommendation.} Either:
(a) Add 8--10 experiments at intermediate PS values (5--25\,nm) to create
a bridge between the mesopore and macropore regimes, or
(b) Treat macropore and mesopore data as separate sub-domains and build
separate models.

\subsection{Priority 4: Densify Temperature Coverage}

\textbf{Rationale.} Temperature is the least important feature (importance
= 0.035) and its range is dominated by experiments at 25\,°C ($>$75\,\% of
data in the IQR [25, 30]\,°C).  Three outliers at 45--55\,°C are isolated.

\textbf{Recommendation.} Add experiments at 35, 40, and 45\,°C to fill
the gap, enabling the model to learn a continuous temperature response.

\section{Summary and Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]

  \item \textbf{Performance saturation is data-limited, not
        model-limited.}  Systematic HPO across five model families
        (Ridge, Lasso, SVR, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) with
        5-fold CV selected Gradient Boosting as the top-ranked architecture.
        However, the difference between GB and RF is \textbf{not
        statistically significant} (Wilcoxon $p = 0.784$ on RMSE,
        $p = 0.920$ on MAE).  The HPO-selected model achieves a mean test
        RMSE of $311.0 \pm 127.7$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ (MAE: $212.0 \pm 87.1$)
        on the 10-trial protocol.

  \item \textbf{The evaluation variance is inherently large.}  With only
        37 training and 10 test samples per trial, the 95\,\% bootstrap
        CI spans $\pm$24\,\% of the mean RMSE.  Differences of
        $<$30\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ between model configurations are
        statistically insignificant at $p < 0.05$.

  \item \textbf{Significant heteroscedasticity.}  Prediction errors are
        significantly correlated with target magnitude (Spearman $\rho =
        0.327$, $p = 0.025$), with Q4 MAE (442\,mg\,g$^{-1}$) being
        2.8$\times$ Q1 MAE (158).  RMSE is therefore dominated by the
        under-sampled high-value tail.  MAE provides a more balanced
        view of model accuracy.

  \item \textbf{Test-set composition drives apparent performance.}
        Performance varies mainly due to
        whether high-value extreme samples land in the test set.

  \item \textbf{The high-performance region is severely under-sampled.}
        Only 6 samples (10.7\,\%) have adsorption capacity
        $>$993\,mg\,g$^{-1}$.  This tail is the primary source of
        high-RMSE trials and the region most relevant to practical
        adsorbent design.

  \item \textbf{Targeted data collection is 3--4× more efficient than
        random augmentation.}  The power-law exponent of $-0.17$ under
        random sampling implies diminishing returns; targeted sampling
        in under-represented regions is expected to recover the
        functional form of the high-adsorption regime more efficiently.
        \emph{Caveat:} the power-law extrapolation is fitted on only 7
        points ($n \in [15, 37]$) and extrapolation to $n > 100$ is
        speculative.

  \item \textbf{Recommended minimum additional experiments: $\approx 40$}
        (Phase 1), with a longer-term target of 120 additional experiments
        to reach sub-200\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ RMSE (Phase 3).

\end{enumerate}

\section{Statistical Limitations and Caveats}
\label{sec:limitations}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This section consolidates the statistical limitations identified throughout
the analysis, organised by the taxonomy of the external review.

\subsection{Model Comparison is Not Statistically Conclusive}

The difference between the top two models (GB vs.\ RF) is
$\Delta\text{RMSE} = +12.0$ (Dataset~4) and $-7.5$ (Dataset~5) —
both with Wilcoxon $p > 0.3$.  With only 10 trials and CV $> 0.4$,
meaningful model discrimination requires either (a)~substantially
more data, or (b)~repeated $k$-fold CV with $\geq$30 repetitions.
The model ranking is \emph{plausible} but not \emph{proven}.

\subsection{RMSE Under Heavy Skew}

The target skewness of $\sim$2.1--2.3 means RMSE is dominated by
high-value samples.  We report MAE alongside RMSE throughout; however,
quantile-specific losses and log-space metrics would provide additional
perspectives.  Log-space RMSE on LOO-CV is 0.950 (Dataset~4) and 0.852
(Dataset~5).

\subsection{LOO-CV Estimator Variance}

LOO-CV is deterministic but \textbf{high-variance as an estimator} of
expected prediction error due to highly correlated training folds
\citep{Hastie2009}.  Its R$^2$ (0.514/0.838) should be interpreted
cautiously: single-sample test folds make R$^2$ unstable and potentially
misleading.  The LOO-CV RMSE should not be directly compared with the
10-trial RMSE as they measure different quantities.

\subsection{Learning Curve Extrapolation}

The power-law fit is based on 7 points over a narrow range
($n \in [15, 37]$).  Extrapolation to $n > 100$ is speculative;
the fitted exponent may change as the training set grows.  Projected
sample sizes (e.g., ``1,124 for RMSE $\leq$200'') should be treated
as rough order-of-magnitude estimates.

\subsection{Data Leakage Audit}

\textbf{Normalization:}  \texttt{StandardScaler} is fitted on
\texttt{X\_train} only and applied via \texttt{transform} to validation
and test sets — no leakage.  \checkmark

\textbf{HPO:}  Grid search uses 5-fold CV on the train+val pool; the
held-out test set is never used during HPO.  \checkmark

\textbf{Feature selection:}  Not applicable (all features used).  \checkmark

\textbf{LOO-CV scaling:}  The scaler is fitted once on the full train+val
pool rather than re-fitted per LOO fold.  This introduces a minor
leakage of the left-out sample's contribution to the mean/std
($\sim$1/$n$ effect).  For $n \geq 40$, the impact on RMSE is
$< 1$\,\% and is considered negligible.  \textcolor{orange}{$\sim$}

\subsection{Missing Analyses}

The following analyses are \emph{not} included in this report and
represent directions for future work:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Prediction intervals and uncertainty quantification:}
        All predictions are point estimates.  Conformal prediction or
        Bayesian approaches (e.g., NGBoost, quantile regression forests)
        would provide per-sample uncertainty bounds.
  \item \textbf{External validation:}  All evaluation is internal
        (CV-based).  No held-out dataset from an independent laboratory
        or experimental campaign is available.
  \item \textbf{SHAP stability analysis:}  Permutation importance is
        computed on the full pool; SHAP values across folds would reveal
        whether feature attributions are stable.
  \item \textbf{Formal heteroscedasticity correction:}  A weighted
        loss function or target transformation (e.g., Box--Cox) could
        improve tail predictions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Nyquist Sampling Analysis and Upper-Bound Estimation}
\label{sec:nyquist}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The \emph{Nyquist--Shannon sampling theorem} \citep{Nyquist1928, Shannon1949} states that a band-limited
function can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling
density is at least twice the highest spatial frequency present in the
function.  We adapt this framework to the tabular regression setting by
treating the target function $y(\mathbf{x})$ as a spatial signal over the
7-dimensional feature space.  As shown by \citet{Balestriero2021}, in
high-dimensional spaces the training data is inherently sparse and
learning almost always amounts to extrapolation, making sampling density
a first-order concern.

\subsection{Spatial Frequency of the Target Function}

The local \emph{spatial gradient} $|\Delta y|/|\Delta x|$ (in
mg\,g$^{-1}$ per normalised feature unit) quantifies how rapidly the
target changes relative to the sampling density.  Using all 1\,540
sample pairs we compute:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Median gradient: $139.4$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$/unit
  \item 95th-percentile gradient: $1{,}316$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$/unit
  \item Maximum gradient (closest high-$\Delta y$ pair): $1{,}708$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$/unit
\end{itemize}

The variogram (Table~\ref{tab:variogram}) shows that $\gamma(h)$ rises
steeply from zero and reaches 80\,\% of the sill at $h \approx 2.37$
(normalised units), defining the \emph{spatial range} of the target
function — the distance beyond which pairs of samples become practically
uncorrelated \citep{Webster2007}.  The near-zero nugget ($\gamma(0) \approx 0$) indicates no
significant measurement noise; all 311\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ of current model
error is \emph{reducible} in principle with better data coverage.

\subsection{Nyquist Spacing Requirement}

For a function of effective dimensionality $d_{\text{eff}} = 2.9$ defined
over a feature space of span $L = 6$ (in normalised units), the sampling
density required to achieve a target RMSE of $\epsilon_0$ can be estimated
via:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{2\epsilon_0}{\bar{f}}, \qquad
  n_{\text{required}} \approx \left(\frac{L}{\delta_{\text{Nyquist}}}\right)^{d_{\text{eff}}}
  \label{eq:nyquist_n}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{f} = 139.4$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$/unit is the median spatial
frequency.  Table~\ref{tab:nyquist_req} shows the implied sample sizes.

\subsection{High-Gradient Region: Local Nyquist Analysis}

The 28 sample pairs with distance $< 2.0$ and $|\Delta y| > 500$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$
define a \emph{high-gradient sub-region} of the input space where the
target function changes most rapidly.  The centroid of this region
corresponds to the transition between moderate-SSA / low-concentration
adsorbents (e.g., $y \approx 200$--$500$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$) and high-SSA /
high-concentration systems (e.g., $y \approx 1{,}700$--$2{,}250$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$).

The maximum local gradient in this region is $1{,}461$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$/unit
(pair samples 18 and 42, distance 1.38).  To satisfy the Nyquist condition
in this sub-region and achieve local interpolation RMSE $< 50$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\text{high-grad}} < \frac{2 \times 50}{1{,}461} \approx 0.068
\end{equation}
The current average spacing in this region is $\approx 1.62$ — a factor
of $\sim$24 coarser than Nyquist.  To close this gap with uniform coverage
of the region requires approximately $24^{2.9} \approx 12{,}000$ samples
per unit volume, a number that is clearly impractical.  However, even a
modest 10-fold reduction (spacing $\approx 0.16$) would reduce local
interpolation error by an order of magnitude.

\subsection{Theoretical Minimum Achievable RMSE}

Combining the topological and Nyquist analyses, we can estimate upper and
lower bounds on the achievable RMSE:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Hard lower bound (nugget):} The semivariogram nugget
        $\gamma(0) \approx 0$ indicates no irreducible measurement noise.
        In principle, RMSE could approach zero with sufficiently dense
        data.
  \item \textbf{Data-density upper bound ($k$-NN oracle):}
        With the \emph{current} 47 train+val samples, the best any
        interpolation model can achieve is RMSE $\approx 328$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$
        (1-NN LOO).  The best GB model achieves 311\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ (10-trial)
        or 368\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ (LOO), in either case close to the oracle.
  \item \textbf{Connectivity constraint:} The feature-space graph requires
        $\varepsilon = 7.3$ to connect, meaning there are genuine voids
        where any model must extrapolate rather than interpolate.  The
        isolated sample~30 (NN distance = 7.24) alone contributes an
        irreducible extrapolation error whenever it appears in a test set.
  \item \textbf{Nyquist bound for target RMSE 100\,mg\,g$^{-1}$:} Requires
        approximately 63 uniformly distributed samples (Table~\ref{tab:nyquist_req}).
        The current 56 samples are non-uniformly distributed and several
        are isolated, so $\sim$100 well-targeted additional experiments
        would be needed in practice.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Dataset~5 yields markedly better prediction performance}
        ($-$40\,\% RMSE, $+$63\,\% $R^2$ on LOO-CV) despite having
        8 fewer samples and 1 fewer feature.  This suggests that the
        ``Equilibrium time'' variable in Dataset~4 introduced noise
        or redundancy rather than useful signal.

  \item \textbf{The learning curve is no longer saturated.}  The steeper
        power-law exponent ($-0.47$ vs.\ $-0.17$) means additional data
        will produce substantial RMSE reductions.  Roughly 50 training
        samples are needed for RMSE~$\leq 200$, compared with
        $\approx 1{,}124$ in Dataset~4.

  \item \textbf{Feature-space topology is more compact.}  Full connectivity
        at $\varepsilon = 4.0$ (vs.\ 7.3) and a maximum NN distance of
        3.42 (vs.\ 7.24) indicate fewer extreme voids, enabling more
        reliable interpolation.

  \item \textbf{SSA is the single most important feature} (importance 0.38,
        Spearman $\rho = 0.820$), followed by Dosage ratio (0.30).
        Together they account for 68\,\% of model importance.

  \item \textbf{A non-zero variogram nugget ($\approx 21{,}000$)} appeared
        in Dataset~5, suggesting modest measurement noise that places
        a fundamental lower bound on achievable RMSE of approximately
        $\sqrt{21{,}000} \approx 146$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ — or lower if the
        nugget is partly attributable to short-range spatial variability.

  \item \textbf{Recommended minimum additional data: 55 targeted experiments}
        (Phase~1+2), with a goal of reaching RMSE~$\approx 100$\,mg\,g$^{-1}$
        at 128 total samples (Phase~3).
\end{enumerate}
